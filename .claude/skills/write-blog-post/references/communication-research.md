# Communication research behind `write-blog-post`

Full sourcing for the rules in `SKILL.md`. Kept separate so the skill itself stays short — read this when you want the "why," not just the rule.

## Reader-value, not writer-value

**Larry McEnerney, Director of the University of Chicago Writing Program — "The Craft of Writing Effectively"** (UChicago Leadership Lab lecture). McEnerney's central claim: writing quality is not primarily about clarity, organization, or persuasiveness — those are downstream of *value to the reader*. A well-organized, clear piece of writing that tells the reader nothing they value is still bad writing. He also notes subject-matter expertise doesn't correlate with writing ability — the two are separate skills. Applied here: Phase 1 forces an explicit answer to "why does the reader care," before structure or prose is touched.

## Scanning behavior on the web

**Nielsen Norman Group, "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading Web Content"** (original eye-tracking study 2006, reaffirmed in follow-up research). Findings: users scan in an F-shaped pattern — a horizontal sweep near the top, a shorter horizontal sweep further down, then a vertical scan down the left edge. On an average page, people read at most ~20–28% of the words present; the rest is skipped. Practical implication NN/g draws directly: put information-carrying words first in headings, subheads, and the opening of paragraphs, because the first word or two of a line gets disproportionate attention in the vertical scan. This is the basis for the Phase 2 lead/callout placement and the subheading rule in Phase 4.

## Sentence-level emphasis: topic and stress position

**George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing," *American Scientist* 78(6):550–558 (Nov–Dec 1990).** Gopen (Duke University) and Swan describe two structural positions every reader unconsciously expects: the **topic position** (start of a sentence/clause), where readers expect context and whoever/whatever the sentence is "about," and the **stress position** (end of a sentence/clause, at a point of syntactic closure), where readers place the greatest emphasis and expect the point that matters most. A writer who puts old, familiar information in the topic position and the new, emphatic information in the stress position aligns the reader's natural emphasis with the writer's intended emphasis. Misaligning the two is a common, invisible cause of prose that feels "hard to follow" even though every sentence is grammatically correct.

## Cognitive load and chunking

**John Sweller, "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning," *Cognitive Science* 12(2) (1988), and subsequent Cognitive Load Theory work.** Sweller (UNSW) distinguishes intrinsic load (inherent difficulty of the material), extraneous load (load added by poor presentation), and germane load (effort spent building understanding). Working memory is limited; instructional/expository material that reduces extraneous load — via chunking, short units, and eliminating unnecessary complexity — leaves more working-memory capacity for actually understanding the content. Applied here: the 2–5 sentence paragraph cap and "one idea per section" rule are extraneous-load reduction, not arbitrary style.

## Making the core idea memorable

**Chip Heath (Stanford Graduate School of Business) and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die* (Random House, 2007).** The SUCCESs framework: **S**imple (find the single core message — a good idea sacrificed for a comprehensive one is a common failure mode), **U**nexpected, **C**oncrete, **C**redible, **E**motional, **S**tories. This skill leans hardest on the first principle, Simple — Phase 1's "one idea" requirement — since a personal blog post has no editor forcing scope discipline the way a commissioned piece would.

## Curse of knowledge and classic style

**Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style: The Thinking Person's Guide to Writing in the 21st Century* (Viking, 2014).** Pinker (Harvard) names the "curse of knowledge" — a writer's difficulty imagining what it's like *not* to know something they know — as, in his words, "the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose." Writers under the curse skip defining jargon, skip logical steps that feel obvious to them, and describe scenes without the detail a reader who wasn't there needs. Pinker also endorses "classic style": a writer in conversation with a reader, pointing at something in the world, as opposed to a defensive, hedge-everything academic register. Applied here: the first-use jargon rule and the first-person, conversational voice requirement.

## Clarity and cutting clutter

**William Zinsser, *On Writing Well: The Classic Guide to Writing Nonfiction* (Harper & Row, 1976; many editions since).** Zinsser's recurring claim: "Clutter is the disease of American writing... unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills and meaningless jargon." His prescription is aggressive cutting — every word, phrase, or qualifier that doesn't earn its place goes — and reading a draft aloud as a practical test, since spoken stumbles reveal written clutter. Both are in the Phase 4 checklist directly.

## Reading speed and the length cap

**Marc Brysbaert, "How many words do we read per minute? A review and meta-analysis of reading rate," *Journal of Memory and Language* 109 (2019).** Brysbaert (Ghent University) pooled 190 studies covering 18,573 participants and found an average adult silent reading speed of 238 words per minute for non-fiction text (175–300 wpm for most adults). This is the basis for translating the Phase 3 word-count cap into a reading-time estimate, and for treating "reading time" as a real, bounded resource rather than an arbitrary number.

## Plain, reader-first language

**Plain Writing Act of 2010 / Federal Plain Language Guidelines (plainlanguage.gov).** U.S. federal guidance for public-facing writing: write for the reader (not the writer), state the major point first, use active voice, use everyday words and define technical terms on first use, keep sentences short, and cut unneeded words. These overlap heavily with Zinsser and Pinker above and back the plain-language default in Phase 2 and Phase 4.

## Deliberately excluded: SEO length research

Several content-marketing sources (Backlinko-style studies, agency blogs) claim 1,500–3,000+ word posts perform best for search ranking and engagement. That research measures a different goal — organic search performance for commercial content — not reader comprehension or satisfaction, and the sources disagree with each other on the specifics. It's excluded from this skill on purpose: optimizing a personal blog's length for search ranking would fight directly against the cognitive-load and scanning research above. See Phase 3 of `SKILL.md` for the explicit "don't do this" rule.
